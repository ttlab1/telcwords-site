German Articles der, die, das: Rules by Ending

Every German noun has a gender. It is masculine (der), feminine (die) or neuter (das). The gender is not only about the word "the". It also changes the ending of adjectives and the pronoun you use later: der Tisch (table) becomes "er", die Zeitung (newspaper) becomes "sie", das Auto (car) becomes "es". So a wrong article often leads to more mistakes in the same sentence.

Many guides give you a list of "rules". We wanted to know how well those rules really work. So we took all the nouns from the TELC Words lists for A1, A2, B1, B2 and C1 and counted. This page shows the results: which endings you can trust, which ones are only a hint, and which exceptions you should learn by heart.

Quick answer: the rules you can trust most

  • die: nouns ending in -ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -ion, -tät. In our lists these had no exceptions at all (258 nouns with -ung alone).
  • die: -ie (29 of 30) and -ik (22 of 24).
  • der: days, months and seasons (23 in our lists), and nouns ending in -ig / -ich (13 of 13).
  • das: small things with -chen (every real diminutive in our lists), -ment (8 of 9) and -um when it is not -aum (23 of 24).
  • No rule? Learn the noun with its article. That works for every word.

How common are der, die and das?

Our lists contain 2,394 different nouns with a single gender (A1 to C1 together, each noun counted once). This is how they split:

der 822 (34.3%)   die 1,073 (44.8%)   das 499 (20.8%)

So die is the most common article, and das is the least common. But the mix changes with the level. At A1, der is the biggest group (41.8%, die 35.7%). At C1, die is clearly ahead (58.5%, der only 21.1%). The reason is simple: advanced words are often abstract nouns like die Gesellschaft (society) or die Möglichkeit (possibility), and their endings are feminine.

How we counted: we pooled all nouns marked der, die or das in the five TELC Words lists and removed duplicates. We left out multi-word entries (like "duale Ausbildung"), nouns shown as der/die (people) and plural-only words. No noun appeared with two different genders. All numbers on this page are about our word lists, not about the whole German language.

About -e: about nine in ten nouns ending in -e are feminine, so "die" is a good guess. The exceptions come in groups that are easy to spot. Male people and animals take der: der Junge (boy), der Kollege (colleague), der Kunde (customer), der Hase (hare / rabbit). A few common words take der: der Name (name) (and der Vorname (first name)), der Käse (cheese), der Gedanke (thought). A few short words ending in -ee are der: der Kaffee (coffee), der Tee (tea), der Schnee (snow). And a short list is das: das Ende (end), das Auge (eye), das Interesse (interest), das Gebäude (building).

About -in: the ending -in is feminine when it makes a female person, like die Sekretärin (secretary). But many nouns just happen to end in -in, and they are not feminine: der Termin (appointment), der Wein (wine), das Benzin (gas). That is why the number in the table is low. Use this rule only for women's job titles and family words.

About -er: this is the rule that fails most often. Only about two in three nouns ending in -er are masculine. Family words are feminine (die Mutter (mother), die Tochter (daughter), die Schwester (sister)), and many everyday things are neuter (das Zimmer (room), das Fenster (window), das Wasser (water)). The rule gets much better when the noun is made from a verb: fahren → der Fahrer (driver), mieten → der Mieter (tenant), stecken → der Stecker (electrical plug). These are people or things that do something, and they are almost always der.

About -ling, -ist, -ant: our lists have only a few nouns with these endings. The exceptions are not real exceptions to the rule: das Controlling (controlling) and das Mailing (mailing) are English -ing words, die Kündigungsfrist (notice period) ends in the word "Frist", and das Restaurant (restaurant) comes from French. We found only one noun ending in -ismus (der Mechanismus (mechanism)), so it is not in the table.

About -chen: -chen makes a word small (a diminutive), and these -chen words are always das. That is why it is das Mädchen (girl), even though a girl is female. der Kuchen (cake) only looks like a diminutive, so it does not follow the rule. We found no nouns ending in -lein in our lists.

About -um: if you count every noun ending in -um, the rule looks weak (23 of 35). But the exceptions are almost all words with -aum, like der Raum (room), der Baum (tree) and der Traum (dream). Leave those out and -um is a strong das rule.

About Ge-: many textbooks say that nouns starting with Ge- are often neuter. In our lists that is not true: only 40% of them are das (29 of 72), and slightly more of them are die. Many of them simply follow a stronger ending, like die Gesundheit (health) (-heit) or die Gesellschaft (society) (-schaft). Do not rely on this one.

Rules by meaning

Days, months and seasons are der

All 23 days, months and seasons in our lists are masculine: der Montag (Monday), der Mai (May), der Sommer (summer), der Winter (winter). Learn this once and you know all of them.

Verbs used as nouns are das

When you use the infinitive of a verb as a noun, it is always neuter: das Essen (food / meal), das Leben (life), das Wissen (knowledge), das Fernsehen (television). This is very useful because you can do it with almost any verb.

People usually follow their sex, but grammar wins

Words for men are normally der and words for women are normally die: der Mann (man), die Frau (woman / wife). But endings are stronger than meaning. das Mädchen (girl) is das because of -chen, and das Kind (child) is also das.

Plurals always use die

In the plural, every noun takes die, whatever its gender in the singular. Some words only exist in the plural, and our lists show them that way: die Eltern (parents), die Leute (people).

Compound nouns: look at the last part

German builds long nouns from short ones. The gender almost always comes from the last part. So if you know the short word, you know the long one.

Watch out for das Teil (part): in our lists it is das, but der Vorteil (advantage) and der Nachteil (disadvantage) are der. The word Teil can be der or das, depending on the meaning, so learn these two as they are.

Common exceptions worth learning by heart

These words break a rule from the tables above. They are all in our A1–B1 lists, so you will meet them early.

  • das Mädchen (girl): a girl, but -chen makes it das.
  • der Name (name), der Käse (cheese), der Junge (boy): end in -e but are der.
  • das Ende (end), das Auge (eye), das Interesse (interest): end in -e but are das.
  • die Mutter (mother), die Tochter (daughter), die Butter (butter), die Nummer (number): end in -er but are die.
  • das Zimmer (room), das Fenster (window), das Wasser (water), das Wetter (weather): end in -er but are das.
  • der Termin (appointment), der Wein (wine): end in -in but are der.
  • der Moment (moment): ends in -ment but is der.
  • die Erlaubnis (permission): ends in -nis but is die.
  • der Euro (euro): ends in -o but is der.
  • die Firma (company): ends in -ma but is die.

Nouns for people: der/die in our lists

Many nouns for people have a male and a female form. Our lists show 59 of these as der/die, for example der/die Arbeitgeber (employer). For most of them, the female form adds -in. Some words come from adjectives and keep the same form for both: der/die Erwachsene (adult), der/die Angestellte (employee). For these words, learn both articles.

How to practise der, die and das

  1. Always learn the noun with its article. Do not learn "Tisch". Learn "der Tisch". Say it out loud.
  2. Use colour. In TELC Words, der is blue, die is pink and das is green. After a while, the colour comes back to you together with the word.
  3. Use the endings first. Before you guess, look at the end of the word. If it is -ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -ion or -tät, you are done.
  4. Collect your exceptions. Write the nouns you get wrong on one short list and look at it every day.
  5. Keep sessions short. A few minutes every day is easier to keep up than one long session per week.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a rule for der, die, das?

There is no single rule, but some endings are very reliable. In the TELC Words lists (2,394 nouns from A1 to C1), every noun ending in -ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -ion or -tät is feminine. Many other nouns follow no clear pattern, so learn each noun together with its article.

Which article is most common in the TELC Words lists?

die is the most common article in our lists (44.8% of nouns), then der (34.3%) and das (20.8%). At A1, der is the largest group (41.8%). These numbers describe our word lists, not the whole German language.

Are nouns ending in -er always masculine?

No. In our lists only 69% of nouns ending in -er are der. Nouns made from a verb, like der Fahrer (driver), are almost always der. Common exceptions are die Mutter, die Nummer, das Zimmer and das Wasser.

What article do plural nouns take?

All plural nouns take die, whatever their gender in the singular. That is one more reason to learn the singular article: the plural does not tell you the gender.

Why do articles matter for telc exam preparation?

Gender decides the article, the adjective ending and the pronoun. So it shows in almost everything you write and say in German. If you learn every noun with its article from A1 onwards, you avoid many small mistakes later.
