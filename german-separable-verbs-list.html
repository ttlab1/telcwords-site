German Separable Verbs List: 138 Verbs from A1 to B1

Some German verbs come apart in a sentence. You learn anrufen (to call), but you say "Ich rufe dich morgen an". The little word an jumps to the end. These are called separable verbs (in German: trennbare Verben), and they are everywhere in everyday German, from the first week of an A1 course.

Most lists online give you the same fifty verbs. This page is different: it shows every separable verb in the TELC Words A1, A2 and B1 word lists, grouped by prefix, with the English meaning from our lists and the level where each verb first appears. Above the list you find the rules you need: where the prefix goes, how the Perfekt works and how to hear whether a verb is separable.

Quick answer

  • Our A1–B1 lists contain 138 separable verbs: 18 first appear at A1, 67 at A2 and 53 at B1.
  • The most common prefixes are an- (20), ab- (19), aus- (19), ein- (15) and auf- (14). These five cover 87 of the 138 verbs (63%).
  • In a main clause the prefix goes to the end: Ich stehe um sieben Uhr auf.
  • In the Perfekt, ge goes in the middle: angerufen, aufgestanden.
  • Be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-, emp-, ent- and miss- never separate.

What is a separable verb?

A separable verb has two parts: a prefix and a normal verb. einkaufen (to shop) is ein + kaufen. aufstehen (to get up) is auf + stehen. The prefix often changes the meaning a lot, so a separable verb is really a new word, not just the basic verb with a small extra.

The prefix is usually a short word that also exists on its own, mostly a preposition or an adverb: an, auf, aus, ein, mit, nach, vor, zu, weg, zurück. Some separable verbs start with an adjective or another word, like hochladen (to upload) or wehtun (to hurt). In the list below, the prefix is shown in colour and separated with a dot: anrufen, aufstehen, zurückgeben.

Where does the prefix go?

The rule depends on the type of sentence. Here are the five situations you meet from A1 to B1.

1. Main clauses, questions and commands: prefix at the end

The verb goes in its normal place (second position, or first in a yes/no question or a command). The prefix goes to the very end of the clause. Everything else stands between the two parts, like inside a bracket.

  • Ich rufe dich morgen an.I will call you tomorrow.
  • Der Zug fährt um acht Uhr ab.The train departs at eight.
  • Wann stehst du auf?When do you get up?
  • Mach bitte das Fenster zu!Please close the window.

2. With a modal verb: the verb stays together at the end

With können, müssen, wollen, dürfen, sollen or möchten, the modal verb takes second position. The separable verb goes to the end as one word, in the infinitive.

  • Ich muss morgen früh aufstehen.I have to get up early tomorrow.
  • Kannst du mich vom Bahnhof abholen?Can you pick me up from the station?

3. In a subordinate clause: the verb stays together at the end

After weil, dass, wenn, ob and similar words, the conjugated verb goes to the end of the clause. The prefix does not split off. It stays at the front of the verb.

  • Ich bin müde, weil ich jeden Tag um sechs Uhr aufstehe.I am tired because I get up at six every day.
  • Sie sagt, dass der Kurs am Montag anfängt.She says that the course starts on Monday.

4. In the Perfekt: ge goes in the middle

The past participle puts ge between the prefix and the verb. As with other verbs, some take haben and some take sein (ich bin aufgestanden, ich bin angekommen).

  • Ich habe dich gestern angerufen.I called you yesterday.
  • Wir sind um sieben Uhr aufgestanden.We got up at seven.
  • Hast du schon eingekauft?Have you already done the shopping?

More examples from the list: abholen → abgeholt, fernsehen → ferngesehen, teilnehmen → teilgenommen, herunterladen → heruntergeladen, stattfinden → stattgefunden.

One exception: when the verb part itself starts with an inseparable prefix, no extra ge is added. vorbereiten (to prepare) becomes vorbereitet, and zubereiten (to prepare) becomes zubereitet.

5. With zu: zu goes in the middle

When you need an infinitive with zu (after "Ich habe keine Zeit", "Vergiss nicht", "um … zu" and so on), the zu also goes between the prefix and the verb, and everything is written as one word.

  • Vergiss nicht, das Formular auszufüllen.Do not forget to fill in the form.
  • Ich gehe in die Stadt, um einzukaufen.I am going into town to do some shopping.

The hearing clue: stress on the prefix

In a separable verb, the stress is always on the prefix: ANrufen, AUFstehen, EINkaufen, MITbringen. In an inseparable verb, the stress is on the verb part: beKOMmen, verSTEhen. This is useful when you listen and when you read aloud: if the first syllable is strong and it is a word like an, auf or mit, the verb will probably split.

Inseparable prefixes: be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-, emp-, ent-, miss-

These eight prefixes never split off, never carry the stress and take no ge- in the Perfekt. They are not words on their own, which helps you recognise them. Examples from our lists:

  • be-: bekommen (to receive) → Ich habe eine E-Mail bekommen. Also bezahlen (to pay), besuchen (to visit).
  • ver-: verstehen (to understand) → Hast du das verstanden? Also vergessen (to forget).
  • er-: erklären (to explain), erzählen (to tell).
  • ge-: gehören (to belong), gefallen (to like).
  • emp- and ent-: empfehlen (to recommend), entscheiden (to decide).
  • zer- and miss-: zerstören (to destroy), missverstehen (to misunderstand).

A false friend to watch: bekommen means "to receive", not "to become". "To become" is werden.

über-, um-, unter-, durch-: separable or not?

These four prefixes can go both ways. Some verbs with them split, others do not, and a few exist in both versions with different meanings. You cannot tell from the spelling, so learn each verb with one example sentence. The stress helps again: stressed prefix means separable.

  • Separable: umsteigen (to change (transport)) → Ich steige in Köln um. / Ich bin in Köln umgestiegen.
  • Inseparable: übersetzen (to translate) → Sie hat den Text übersetzt. And unterschreiben (to sign) → Ich habe den Vertrag unterschrieben.

Because of this, we did not put verbs with these prefixes in the list below. Our A1–B1 lists contain 26 of them, for example umziehen, übernehmen and unterstützen. You can find them in the full A2 and B1 lists.

How to learn separable verbs for the exam

  1. Learn the common prefix meanings. Many prefixes have a typical meaning, and it often fits: aus- is often "out" (aussteigen (to get off), ausziehen (to move out)), ein- is often "in" (einsteigen (to board), einziehen (to move in)), zurück- means "back" (zurückgeben (to give back)), mit- means "along" (mitbringen (to bring along), mitkommen (to come along)) and weg- means "away" (weggehen (to go away)).
  2. Learn opposite pairs together. anmachen (to turn on) and ausmachen (to switch off); aufmachen (to open) and zumachen (to close); einsteigen (to board) and aussteigen (to get off); abnehmen (to lose weight / take off) and zunehmen (to gain weight).
  3. Do not trust the prefix too much. The meaning is not always predictable. anfangen (to start), aufhören (to stop), ausgeben (to spend), aufpassen (to pay attention) and einfallen (to occur) are hard to guess from their parts. Learn these as new words.
  4. Learn three forms at once. For every verb, say the infinitive, a short main clause and the Perfekt: "anrufen – ich rufe an – ich habe angerufen". This trains the word order at the same time as the word.
  5. Practise speed. In the TELC Words game you get ten seconds per word. Quick recall of verbs like abholen or ankommen helps you understand spoken German, where the prefix comes at the end of a long sentence.

Frequently asked questions

What is a separable verb in German?

A separable verb is a verb with a prefix that splits off in some sentences. In "Ich rufe dich an", the verb anrufen (to call) is split: rufe is in second position and an goes to the end of the clause. In the infinitive, in subordinate clauses and in the Perfekt the prefix stays attached: anrufen, weil ich anrufe, angerufen.

How many separable verbs are in the TELC Words A1 to B1 lists?

We counted 138 different separable verbs: 18 first appear in the A1 list, 67 in A2 and 53 in B1. The most common prefixes are an- (20 verbs), ab- (19) and aus- (19). We left out verbs with über-, um-, unter- and durch-, because these prefixes can be separable or inseparable.

How do I know if a German verb is separable?

Look at the prefix and listen to the stress. Prefixes like an-, auf-, aus-, ein-, mit- and zurück- are separable, and the stress is on the prefix: ANrufen. The prefixes be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-, emp-, ent- and miss- are never separable, and the stress is on the stem: verSTEhen. Über-, um-, unter- and durch- can be either, so learn those verbs one by one.

How do you form the Perfekt of a separable verb?

Put ge- between the prefix and the verb: anrufen becomes angerufen, aufstehen becomes aufgestanden, einkaufen becomes eingekauft. Inseparable verbs take no ge- at all: bekommen stays bekommen, verstehen becomes verstanden.
